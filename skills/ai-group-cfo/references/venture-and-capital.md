# Venture Portfolio, Investment Decisions and Capital Allocation

Read this when the question involves startups the group has built or backed, a new investment, a fundraise, or competing uses of cash.

## Treat venture building as a portfolio

Venture activity is portfolio investment, not ordinary operating expenditure. Buried inside opex, it distorts group margins and hides the fact that capital is being allocated without a return test.

Maintain a portfolio register covering, per company: startup; incorporation date; jurisdiction; ownership; voting rights; invested capital; committed capital; follow-on commitments; cash; monthly burn; runway; revenue; ARR/MRR; gross margin; valuation; last financing; investor; pre-money valuation; post-money valuation; dilution; option pool; liquidation preferences where known; next financing; expected capital requirement; strategic value; probability of success; potential exit.

## Do not confuse these values

Keep strictly distinct: **book value** · **investment carrying value** · **latest financing valuation** · **estimated fair value** · **potential exit value** · **strategic value**.

A startup's last round is evidence of value, not automatically its current economic value — particularly where the round was small, insider-led, priced for signalling reasons, or carries preferences that change what common equity is actually worth.

## Portfolio capital allocation

For each venture, reach one of: **invest more · maintain · restructure · fundraise · merge · divest · wind down**.

Assess: expected return; probability of success; downside; additional capital required; strategic value; opportunity cost; dilution; time to liquidity.

The correct decision is sometimes to stop investing in a company whose revenue is growing. Growth funded by capital that will never earn its cost is value destruction with good optics.

## Investment committee mode

When evaluating an investment, switch into this structure:

- **Investment thesis** — why could this become valuable?
- **Evidence** — what proves the thesis?
- **Economics** — revenue, margins, unit economics, cash
- **Market** — TAM, growth, competition
- **Team** — founder quality and execution capability
- **Capital structure** — cap table, dilution, preferred terms, future funding needs
- **Valuation** — current implied valuation and a reasonable range
- **Risks** — what can destroy the thesis?
- **Downside** — what happens if the plan fails?
- **Upside** — what happens if execution is strong?
- **Recommendation** — INVEST · INVEST WITH CONDITIONS · WAIT · REJECT

Never recommend an investment purely because the company is growing quickly. State the conditions explicitly where the recommendation is conditional — they are the substance of the decision.

## Fundraising mode

When assessing a raise, calculate: amount required; minimum raise; target raise; runway before; runway after; valuation; dilution; use of funds; the milestones the capital buys; and the next financing requirement after this one.

Always answer: **what does this capital buy?**

$3M does not buy "18 months of runway." It buys measurable milestones — an ARR target, a customer count, a product release, a gross-margin improvement, a market entry, a regulatory approval, a profitability milestone. If the raise cannot be expressed that way, the plan is not ready and the next round will be harder to price.

## Capital allocation framework

Every significant use of cash competes against the alternatives: hiring; product development; marketing; acquisitions; venture investments; debt repayment; cash preservation; geographic expansion.

Decide using: expected return · risk · time to return · cash requirement · strategic value · opportunity cost.

Naming the opportunity cost is the part management usually skips. Any proposal evaluated in isolation will look reasonable; the CFO's job is to put it next to what else that money could do.
