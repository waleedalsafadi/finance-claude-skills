# Working Capital, Cash, Planning and Operating Rhythm

Read this for anything involving cash, budgets, forecasts, variances, KPI definitions, or how the finance function should run its month.

## Working capital

Monitor: DSO; DPO; receivables aging; payables aging; customer advances; deferred revenue; unbilled revenue; accrued expenses; inventory where applicable; cash conversion cycle.

Ask constantly: **is growth consuming cash?** A growing company with poor collections can become less valuable despite higher revenue, and this is invisible in a P&L-only conversation. Where receivables are growing faster than revenue, quantify the cash locked up and name the customers responsible.

## Cash management

Cash gets CFO-level attention, always. Where the data permits, maintain both a **13-week cash flow** and a **12–24 month liquidity forecast**.

Track: opening cash; customer collections; payroll; suppliers; taxes; VAT; CAPEX; investments; debt; financing; venture funding; intercompany transfers.

Calculate: net burn; gross burn; runway; minimum cash; financing gap.

Never equate EBITDA with cash generation. In a group with deferred revenue, project billing milestones and intercompany funding, the gap between the two is usually the most important number in the report.

## Driver-based financial model

Maintain an integrated model linking P&L → balance sheet → cash flow → working capital → funding → valuation. Forecast from operational drivers rather than extrapolating historical financial statements.

Revenue drivers: customers; contracts; users; price; usage; utilisation; conversion; churn; expansion.
Cost drivers: headcount; salary; cloud usage; infrastructure; sales; marketing; vendors; financing.

A forecast that cannot be traced back to a driver cannot be challenged, corrected, or held to account — which is the entire purpose of having one.

## Variance analysis

For actual vs budget vs forecast, answer five questions in order:

1. What changed?
2. Why did it change?
3. What is the financial impact?
4. Is it temporary or structural?
5. What should management do?

Every material variance ends in an action or a conclusion. A variance table with no explanation is not analysis, and it trains management to ignore the report.

## Forecast discipline

Maintain three cases: **base** (most realistic expectation), **downside** (reasonable adverse scenario), **upside** (reasonable outperformance).

For material decisions, show the impact on revenue, gross profit, EBITDA, cash, runway, funding requirement and enterprise value.

Never present a forecast as a fact. Keep visibly separate: actuals · management assumptions · CFO assumptions · external benchmarks · estimates.

## KPI dictionary

Maintain a consistent definition for every KPI in use, covering: name; formula; numerator; denominator; period; source; owner; accounting vs management basis; interpretation; limitations.

Never change a metric definition between periods without flagging the change explicitly and restating the comparative. Silent definitional drift is one of the most common ways management reporting loses credibility with a board.

## Monthly CFO operating rhythm

1. **Close** — validate the monthly financial information
2. **Reconcile** — cash, receivables, payables, intercompany, revenue, major balance-sheet accounts
3. **Diagnose** — actual vs budget vs forecast
4. **Explain** — identify the key drivers
5. **Forecast** — update the rolling forecast and cash outlook
6. **Evaluate** — business-unit and venture performance
7. **Decide** — capital allocation and strategic decisions
8. **Communicate** — CEO, board and investor reporting
9. **Track actions** — check whether previous management actions actually delivered

Step 9 is the one most often skipped and the one that most improves the function over time.

## Decision log

For significant decisions, record: decision; date; alternatives considered; assumptions; financial impact; risks; expected outcome; responsible owner; review date; actual result.

Then compare **expected vs actual**. This feedback loop is what turns a reporting function into a decision-making function — and it exposes systematically optimistic assumptions faster than any other control.
