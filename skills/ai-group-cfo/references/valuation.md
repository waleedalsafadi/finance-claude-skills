# Normalisation and Valuation

Read this for profitability assessment, valuation work, fundraising valuation, or M&A.

## Normalised financial performance

When evaluating profitability, valuation, fundraising or M&A, start from reported results and then identify legitimate normalisation items.

Candidate categories: one-off expenses; unusual legal fees; restructuring; founder-specific expenses; abnormal consulting; non-recurring revenue; discontinued activities; unusual transaction costs; accounting anomalies.

Present the bridge:

**Reported EBITDA → +/- valid adjustments → Normalised EBITDA**

Never add back an expense merely because management dislikes it, and never add back something that will recur in a different form. Every adjustment requires an economic rationale that would survive a buyer's due diligence. Where an adjustment is arguable, show the range with and without it rather than picking the flattering version.

## Choosing a methodology

Select the approach based on business model; maturity; profitability; growth; recurring revenue; cash flow; capital intensity; stage; market conditions; and the availability of genuine comparables.

Approaches to consider: DCF; trading comparables; transaction comparables; EV/Revenue; EV/ARR; EV/EBITDA; revenue multiples; VC method; probability-weighted scenarios; calibration to recent financing; net asset approach.

The 2025 IPEV Guidelines emphasise judgement in selecting valuation techniques and address multiples, DCF, net assets and calibration to recent investment.

Never choose a methodology because it produces the highest number. If the highest and lowest defensible methods diverge widely, that divergence is itself the finding — report it.

## Valuation output

A valuation should normally contain:

- **Enterprise value range** — low / base / high
- **Equity value** — after debt, cash and other relevant claims
- **Methodology** — why this approach fits this business
- **Key assumptions** — growth, margins, discount rate, terminal value, multiple
- **Sensitivity** — how value moves when the major assumptions move
- **Cross-check** — a second method wherever practical
- **Investment conclusion** — whether the implied valuation looks conservative, reasonable, aggressive or unsupported

Never present false precision. A range with named drivers is more useful than a point estimate carried to two decimals.

## Venture and early-stage valuation

For early-stage companies consider, as appropriate: recent financing; comparable transactions; comparable public companies; revenue multiples; ARR multiples; DCF where meaningful; the VC method; scenario analysis; probability-weighted outcomes; dilution; liquidation preferences; capital requirements; downside cases.

Where conventional DCF is unreliable — pre-revenue, unproven unit economics, no forecastable cash flows — say so explicitly and explain why, rather than producing a model whose output is determined entirely by its terminal assumptions.

Do not create meaningless precision. For a seed-stage company, "a defensible range of X–Y, driven mainly by whether the ARR assumption holds" is an honest answer; a single number to the nearest thousand is not.

## Benchmark applicability

Do not use a generic "software multiple." Before applying any comparable, check that the comparable business is actually comparable on growth rate, gross margin, recurring share of revenue, scale, geography and capital structure. Record source, date, sector, geography, stage, metric and methodology for every benchmark used — see `research-and-evidence.md`.
