# Group Structure, Multi-Country Finance and Consolidation

Read this when the work touches more than one legal entity, more than one currency, or the question of what belongs inside the group at all.

## Group master file

Maintain a conceptual master record for every entity in the group covering: legal name; jurisdiction; entity type; ownership; voting rights; control status; parent entity; functional currency; reporting currency; tax jurisdiction; business activity; revenue streams; bank accounts; intercompany relationships; subsidiaries; associates; joint ventures; investments; venture holdings.

When any of these are unknown, note the gap rather than assuming. Ownership percentage is the field most often mistaken for the answer to a question it does not answer.

## Control, not ownership

Do not determine consolidation solely from ownership percentage. Assess control based on the applicable accounting framework and the actual rights and circumstances — shareholder agreements, board composition, veto rights, funding dependence, and who genuinely directs the relevant activities.

Under IFRS, apply IFRS 10 principles when assessing whether an investee should be consolidated. A 40% holding with control consolidates; a 60% holding without it may not.

## Multi-country finance

Treat the group as two things at once: a collection of legal entities, and one economic group. Both readings are needed, and they answer different questions.

Analyse separately: entity performance; country performance; business-line performance; consolidated performance; intercompany activity; FX; taxes; working capital; capital flows.

Determine the appropriate functional currency for each entity rather than defaulting to the group's presentation currency. Where financial statements are translated into another presentation currency, handle foreign operations and translation under the applicable framework — IAS 21 addresses functional currency determination, foreign-currency transactions, and translation of foreign operations.

Never casually convert every country into one currency at one arbitrary rate. In high-inflation or restricted-currency environments this alone can invert the apparent performance of an entity.

## Consolidation engine

When consolidation is relevant, work through: parent/subsidiary relationships; control; non-controlling interests; acquisition dates; intercompany revenue; intercompany costs; intercompany receivables; intercompany payables; intercompany loans; unrealised intercompany profit; dividends; FX translation; consolidation adjustments.

Present the result as a visible bridge:

**Entity results → consolidation adjustments → consolidated results**

Never add entity P&Ls together and call the total group performance. Where intercompany revenue is material, show what group revenue looks like before and after elimination — management frequently reports the pre-elimination figure without realising it.

Watch for intercompany balances that do not agree between entities. Unreconciled intercompany is both a data-quality red flag and, often, a sign of unrecorded transactions somewhere in the group.

## Accounting framework control

Establish the applicable framework before applying any technical treatment: IFRS; IFRS for SMEs; local GAAP; US GAAP; or a management accounting basis. Do not mix frameworks casually, and be explicit when the management basis deliberately departs from the statutory one.

Where IFRS applies, use the relevant IFRS/IAS requirements for consolidation, revenue, leases, intangible assets, financial instruments, FX, impairment, provisions, and taxes.

Where a technical accounting judgement is required, state the judgement, name the relevant standard, and set out the facts it depends on. Do not pretend there is a simple answer, and do not reach an accounting conclusion where the underlying facts are insufficient — say what facts would be needed to conclude.
