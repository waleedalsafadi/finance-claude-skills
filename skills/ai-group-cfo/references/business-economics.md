# Business-Unit Economics

Read this when analysing how the business actually makes money: revenue quality, project delivery, recurring revenue, cloud costs, people, or development spend.

## Business-unit architecture

Separate the group's economics by business model, not by legal entity or department. Typical units in a technology group: app/software development; SaaS; cloud; cybersecurity; IT services; managed services; consulting; system integration; licensing; AI; support and maintenance; project-based services; recurring services; venture building; startup investments.

Do not apply one KPI framework across all of them. A project business and a SaaS business fail in different ways and are valued on different bases. Each business model gets its own economic model.

## Revenue intelligence

For every revenue stream, establish: customer; product/service; contract; pricing model; billing model; revenue recognition basis; recurring vs non-recurring; contracted vs discretionary; gross vs net presentation; direct delivery cost; gross margin; collection profile; working capital impact; scalability; customer concentration.

Keep these strictly separate — they are not interchangeable and management routinely blurs them:

**Revenue** · **Bookings** · **Billings** · **Cash collections** · **Backlog** · **Deferred revenue** · **ARR/MRR**

Gross versus net presentation deserves particular attention where hardware, licences or cloud capacity are resold. Revenue can double with no change in economics.

## Project business

For development, implementation, consulting, cybersecurity and other professional-services work, measure: contract value; recognised revenue; billed amount; collected amount; backlog; remaining delivery cost; estimated gross profit; actual gross profit; project variance; utilisation; billable hours; average billing rate; subcontractor cost; project overruns; change orders; unbilled revenue.

The point of project-level economics is early warning. A deteriorating estimate-to-complete shows up months before it reaches the P&L. Identify loss-making or deteriorating projects early, and use project economics to explain group performance rather than treating them as a separate reporting exercise.

## SaaS and recurring revenue

For recurring businesses maintain a dedicated recurring-revenue layer covering, where applicable: MRR; ARR; new ARR; expansion ARR; contraction; churn; GRR; NRR; logo churn; revenue churn; CAC; CAC payback; LTV; LTV/CAC; gross margin; contribution margin; burn; burn multiple; runway; Rule of 40; revenue per employee; gross profit per employee.

Before calculating any metric, verify its definition and its data source. Never manufacture SaaS metrics from accounting data that cannot support them — an "ARR" figure derived from annualising a month that included one-off project revenue is worse than no figure at all, because it will be quoted to investors.

## Cloud economics

Separate cloud/software revenue from cloud infrastructure economics. Analyse: cloud vendor costs; customer usage; infrastructure utilisation; hosting cost; pass-through costs; customer-specific infrastructure; managed-service costs; gross margin; contract commitments; minimum commitments; reserved capacity; vendor concentration.

Where pass-through revenue materially affects reported revenue, show its impact separately so growth and margin can be read correctly. Minimum commitments and reserved capacity are effectively fixed costs and belong in the runway calculation.

## People economics

Technology businesses are people-intensive; payroll usually dominates the cost base and determines delivery capacity.

Analyse: headcount; payroll; benefits; contractors; utilisation; revenue per employee; gross profit per employee; billable utilisation; delivery capacity; hiring plan; employee productivity.

For every significant hiring decision, work through the full chain rather than the salary alone:

**Cost → Capacity → Revenue potential → Gross profit → Cash impact → Break-even → Runway impact**

State how long the hire takes to reach productivity and what happens to the case if that slips.

## Software development costs

Where development expenditure is material, distinguish: research; development; maintenance; implementation; customisation; internal-use development; customer-specific development.

Do not automatically capitalise development expenditure — capitalisation that flatters EBITDA while consuming the same cash is a red flag, not an improvement. Where IFRS applies, consider the IAS 38 recognition requirements and distinguish qualifying development expenditure from research expenditure.

Where the underlying facts are insufficient to support a capitalisation conclusion, say so and identify what evidence would be needed (technical feasibility, intention and ability to complete, probable future economic benefits, reliable measurement of cost).
